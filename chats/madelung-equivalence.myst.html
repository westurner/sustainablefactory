madelung equivalence

—

googlesearch response

Madelung equivalence refers to the mathematical correspondence between the Schrödinger equation of quantum mechanics and a set of hydrodynamic fluid equations, linked by the Madelung transformation, continuity equation, and quantum potential.

The Madelung Transformation

  • Polar form representation: Writing the complex wave function as \(\psi = \sqrt{\rho} \, e^{iS/\hbar}\), where ρ is probability density and S is phase.

  • Velocity field definition: Fluid velocity v is proportional to the gradient of the phase: \(v = (\hbar/m)\nabla S\).

  • Continuity equation: Realizes conservation of probability mass equivalent to fluid mass conservation.

Hydrodynamic and Quantum Equivalence

  • Quantum Euler equation: The phase evolution maps into a modified Euler equation containing an extra term known as the Bohm quantum potential.

  • Conditional equivalence limits: The hydrodynamic system is strictly equivalent to the Schrödinger equation only if an additional global constraint—such as the quantization of circulation (single-valuedness of the wave function)—is enforced.

  • Applications: Foundational for pilot-wave theories, Bohmian mechanics, and quantum fluid dynamics analogies.

It’s application in non-Bohmian mechanics

—

googlesearch response

While the Madelung transformation is famous for inspiring the de Broglie–Bohm ontology of “hidden particles” guided by waves, its real power lies in Quantum Hydrodynamics (QHD). In non-Bohmian contexts, physicists treat the fluid variables (density \(\rho \)and velocity \(v\)) as describing an actual physical fluid or a collective macroscopic field, rather than just guideposts for a hidden point-particle.

The Madelung equivalence serves as a vital mathematical and physical bridge in several distinct modern applications:

1. Macroscopic Quantum Fluids

  • Superfluidity & Bose-Einstein Condensates (BECs): The Gross-Pitaevskii equation—a nonlinear Schrödinger equation modeling BECs—is regularly analyzed using the Madelung transformation. The resulting fluid equations represent the true macroscopic density and velocity of the superfluid, helping physicists study quantized vortices, sound propagation, and dark solitons.

  • Quantum Plasmas & Electron Gases: QHD is heavily deployed to study dense electron systems and multi-component plasmas. Using Madelung’s fluid picture combined with Poisson’s equation, researchers model nonlocal plasmonics and nonlinear optical responses in two-dimensional electron gases.

2. Quantum Dissipation & Open Systems

  • Stochastic and Open Quantum Dynamics: In open systems where quantum particles interact with a thermal bath, the Madelung decomposition simplifies the coupled equations. It helps map quantum dissipative behaviors into familiar classical frameworks like the Convection-Diffusion, Fokker–Planck, and quantum Boltzmann equations.

3. Cosmic Metamaterials & Scale Analogies

  • Fuzzy Dark Matter (FDM): In astrophysics, certain dark matter candidates are modeled as ultra-light bosons that form a giant cosmic wave function. Cosmologists use the Madelung equations to turn the Schrödinger-Poisson system into fluid dynamics equations. This allows classical cosmological fluid simulation software to compute how these wave-like dark matter structures collapse into halos.

  • Classical Hydrodynamic Analogues: Researchers use walking droplets on vibrating oil baths to visually mimic quantum phenomena like tunneling and orbital quantization. The macroscopic fluid dynamics governing these walking droplets share an equivalent mathematical structure to Madelung’s formulations.

4. Pure Mathematics & Geometry

  • Geometric Hydrodynamics: In pure mathematics, the Madelung transform acts like a transition from Cartesian to polar coordinates on an infinite-dimensional wave function space. Studies in PNAS demonstrate that the transformation acts as an isometry, linking the symplectic structures (the geometry of phase spaces) of classical fluid mechanics directly to quantum physics.

5. Numerical Quantum Simulations

  • Bypassing Complex Wavefunctions: Solving the standard Schrödinger equation for large, highly oscillatory states is computationally taxing. By mapping the wave mechanics into density and velocity fields using the Madelung transformation, engineers can utilize highly optimized, classical computational fluid dynamics (CFD) algorithms to execute quantum simulations.
