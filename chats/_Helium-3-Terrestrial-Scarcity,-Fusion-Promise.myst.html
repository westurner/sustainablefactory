You are technically correct! Helium-3 (He-3) is indeed present in both terrestrial natural gas and the Earth’s oceans. However, when it comes to powering commercial fusion reactors, the catch is the scale and concentration.

Here is the reality of where these resources stand:

Natural Gas Reserves¶

Helium is commonly extracted as a byproduct of natural gas processing. However, the vast majority of this gas is Helium-4, which is constantly generated by the radioactive decay of heavy elements like uranium and thorium in the Earth’s crust.

  • The Abundance: He-3 is a primordial isotope, meaning what is left on Earth was trapped here when the planet formed. In natural gas deposits, He-3 exists only in extreme trace amounts, typically measured in the parts per billion (ppb) relative to He-4.

  • The Viability: While exploration companies have recently found “high-grade” He-3 gas wells (such as the Topaz project in Minnesota, measuring up to 14.5 ppb), harvesting the metric tonnes required to fuel a global fusion grid from these crustal sources remains an enormous economic and engineering hurdle.

Ocean Water¶

He-3 does exist in the oceans, steadily seeping into the water from the Earth’s mantle through deep-sea hydrothermal vents at mid-ocean ridges.

  • The Abundance: The total amount of He-3 in all of the Earth’s oceans combined is estimated to be roughly 13 metric tonnes. Because it is completely dissolved across the entire volume of the global ocean, extracting it would require processing unimaginable quantities of water for an infinitesimally small yield.

  • The “Seawater Fusion” Confusion: When scientists and energy advocates talk about an inexhaustible supply of fusion fuel in ocean water, they are almost always referring to Deuterium (Hydrogen-2), not He-3. Deuterium is incredibly abundant—accounting for roughly 1 in every 6,500 hydrogen atoms in seawater—and is the primary fuel for the standard Deuterium-Tritium (D-T) fusion reactors being developed today.

The Aneutronic Advantage¶

Despite its terrestrial scarcity, He-3 remains a “holy grail” for fusion energy. Reactions like Deuterium-Helium-3 (D-³He) fusion are aneutronic. Instead of producing highly energetic neutrons that degrade reactor walls and create radioactive waste, He-3 fusion produces positively charged protons. These protons can be safely contained by magnetic fields and theoretically used to generate electricity directly, entirely bypassing the need for traditional thermal steam turbines.

Because terrestrial sources in natural gas and the oceans are so extremely diffuse, researchers currently rely on He-3 harvested from the decay of Tritium (from nuclear programs and heavy water reactors) or look toward the long-term prospect of space mining—specifically targeting the lunar regolith, where the solar wind has been depositing He-3 uninterrupted for billions of years.
